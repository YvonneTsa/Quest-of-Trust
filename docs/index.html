01 / THE BUSINESS DECISION

Every flagged payment creates a business choice.

A risk score is only a signal. The policy decides whether to complete, verify, investigate, or stop a payment—balancing fraud exposure, customer effort, payment value, and team capacity.

The business question is simple: when a team cannot investigate every suspicious payment, which approach best balances fraud losses with unnecessary customer interruptions and limited staff time? The comparison uses the same simulated payment activity to assess a simple reference policy, written rules, a logistic regression model, and a combined approach.

Each approach is set using earlier simulated dates and checked on later dates it has not seen. The simulator keeps the fraud labels out of the signals used to make decisions, then reveals those labels only to measure the results.

APPROVEComplete the payment and preserve a smooth checkout; absorb modeled fraud cost if it is fraudulent.
CHALLENGEAsk for extra verification; this may stop fraud but adds effort and cost for legitimate customers too.
REVIEWSend the payment to an investigator; each case uses limited team capacity.
DECLINEStop the payment; a false decline can lose a genuine sale and carries an assumed customer-value cost.

FRAUD EXPOSURE

More value captured on average; not in every run

Logistic captured 77.51% of simulated fraud value versus 73.73% for Rules, a 3.78-point mean lift. The paired difference ranged from −1.86 to +14.31 points. A business should weigh the range, not just the average.

CUSTOMER & TEAM IMPACT

Fewer legitimate payments interrupted

At the median, Logistic sent 4,463.5 fewer legitimate payments per test period to extra verification (range 3,988–5,342 fewer) and 108 fewer to manual review (range 65–138 fewer). That suggests less avoidable customer effort and a lighter queue in this simulation; it does not measure abandonment, complaints, or staff time saved.

MODELED ECONOMICS

A consistent result under the stated assumptions

Logistic had a lower modeled net-cost measure in 30 of 30 runs. The median paired difference was −$15,501.82 per test period, with an observed range from −$22,482.27 to −$11,207.82. These are simulated dollars, not realized savings.

How to read this evidence: The modeled net-cost result favored Logistic in 30/30 seeds, with a paired median difference of −$15,501.82 and an observed range of −$22,482.27 to −$11,207.82. Fraud-capture lift ranged from −1.86 to +14.31 percentage points. These ranges describe variation among simulator runs; they are not confidence intervals or proof of real-world performance. Data, labels, interventions, and dollars are synthetic.
03 / HOW THE TEST WORKS

From simulated payments to a business comparison.

The sequence separates the activity a fraud team could observe from the hidden answer key used only to measure whether the policy caught fraud.

The default scenario represents 2,000 customers, 800 merchants, and 1,200 payment terminals across 60 simulated days—246,314 events in total. It includes ten sampled attack campaigns for each of four patterns. Each later-date test set contains 74,237 events, including 350 simulated fraud cases; the default investigator limit is 250 cases per day. The data and assumptions are documented in the repository.

Explore the business trade-offs

What if the team or costs change?

The dashboard lets you compare saved test results under different daily investigator limits and fictional cost assumptions. Payments that fit within the queue limit go to review; the rest are routed to extra verification. These controls explore scenarios—they do not make live payment decisions or retrain the model.

04 / THE BOUNDARY

What this project can and cannot claim.

The limits matter as much as the findings.

What the project demonstrates

A repeatable way to create a controlled fraud scenario, compare payment policies, make staffing and cost assumptions visible, and explain the trade-offs to decision-makers.

  • No transaction, person, fraud event, cost, or outcome represents a real customer or company.
  • Each later-date test set has 350 simulated fraud cases; the 30 runs use repeated draws from the same fictional setup.
  • The observed ranges show variation across those runs. They are not statistical error bars or evidence of performance on real payments.
  • The simulation does not include linked device, IP address, login, or account-recovery behavior.
  • Differences across fictional groups are descriptive checks, not proof that a real system is fair.
  • The logistic model is an educational comparison, not a production fraud service.
  • Dashboard controls switch among saved scenarios; they do not retrain the model or score live payments.

Business interpretation: The results point to a promising trade-off under the chosen assumptions. A real decision would need representative payment data and validated estimates for fraud losses, verification success, customer abandonment, payment margin, and investigator capacity.
